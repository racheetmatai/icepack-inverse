\begin{abstract}
Uncertainties in basal-sliding parameterizations hinder accurate projections of ice-sheet contributions to sea-level rise. Although inverse methods can calibrate basal friction to reproduce observed velocities, the controls on inversion-derived friction remain poorly understood. We test whether ice and subglacial observables can define a consistent point-wise relationship with the inversion-derived basal-friction control across the Amundsen Sea sector of West Antarctica. Using a sector-wide inversion for the basal-friction control as the reference, we train neural-network regression models in ten experiments, each with a spatially separated 50~km square and buffer withheld from training and validation. We test each friction-control field in a forward simulation against observed velocity. Depending on predictor configuration, the friction fields reduce modeled-velocity error relative to a spatially uniform friction baseline in six to nine of the ten squares. Performance varies among locations, adding predictors does not consistently improve performance, and failures occur even where marginal and joint predictor support is high. Although the tested local predictors improve velocity in most withheld squares, modeled-velocity errors remain substantially larger than for the inversion reference. These results indicate that the considered ice and subglacial observables are insufficient to define a consistently successful point-wise parameterization of inversion-derived basal friction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Ice-sheet models are used to project sea-level rise, reconstruct past ice-sheet behavior, and investigate the physical processes governing ice-mass change \citep[e.g.][]{lecavalier2025history,nowicki2016ice}. These models contain structural uncertainty arising from incomplete representations of physical processes and parameter uncertainty arising from imperfectly constrained model parameters \citep{hebeler2008impact,recinos2023framework}. Both sources of uncertainty limit our ability to constrain probabilistic projections, especially for low-probability, high-impact outcomes \citep{kopp2017evolving}.

Basal slip is an important source of both structural and parameter uncertainty \citep{joughin2019regularized, bulthuis2019uncertainty}. Motion at the ice--bed interface can involve sliding over hard bedrock, deformation of subglacial sediment, and changes in basal water pressure, among other processes \citep{schoof2013ice,cuffey2010physics}. Ice-sheet models generally represent these processes through a sliding law containing one or more spatially varying friction parameters \citep{weertman1964theory,schoof2005cavitation}. Inverse methods adjust these parameters to reduce the mismatch between modeled and observed quantities such as velocity \citep{macayeal1993tutorial}. The resulting friction field therefore absorbs unresolved basal processes, including bed roughness, till rheology, and hydrology \citep{hoffman2022impact,tulaczyk2000basal,lu2023coupling}, as well as geometry errors and differences among model formulations \citep{zhao2018basal,barnes2021transferability}. It is consequently an effective model parameter rather than a direct observation of basal conditions.

Inversion studies show that basal resistance across Antarctica is spatially heterogeneous \citep{morlighem2013inversion,zhao2018basal}. This motivates a working hypothesis: at the resolution of an ice-sheet model, the dimensionless basal-friction control $C(\mathbf{x})$ may be approximated by a point-wise function of observables evaluated at the same location,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:functional_relationship}
C(\mathbf{x}) \approx f\!\left(\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}(\mathbf{x})$ contains locally measured or inferred quantities that may influence basal resistance or serve as proxies for unresolved basal conditions. Equation~\ref{eq:functional_relationship} is a testable point-wise approximation at the model-grid scale, not a physical sliding law. We test it by fitting the relationship outside a held-out region and asking whether the predicted $C$ improves modeled velocity within that region, particularly where the region's predictor values resemble those in the data used for fitting.

We divide the candidate predictors into two groups. The ice-product group describes ice geometry, its gradients, driving stress, and surface air temperature. The subglacial-product group describes bed geometry, bed--surface gradient alignment, geothermal heat flux, and gravity and magnetic anomalies. Ice thickness controls overburden pressure and normal stress at the bed, while surface slope governs driving stress; together, variations in thickness and surface elevation gradient impose systematic constraints on basal drag (\citealp{macayeal1989large}; \citealp[Sections 7.2--7.4 and 8.2]{cuffey2010physics}). Surface air temperature provides the upper thermal boundary condition and influences the ice sheet's englacial and basal thermal state \citep{meierbachtol2015thermal}. 
% Where basal ice reaches the pressure-melting point, basal water pressure can reduce effective pressure and frictional resistance (\citealp[Chapters~6--7]{cuffey2010physics}; \citealp{iken1981effect,dawson2022ice}).

Subglacial conditions can affect basal drag through bed topography and substrate composition. Bed gradients characterize topographic variation that can contribute to form drag \citep{hoffman2022impact}, while deformable till can reduce resistance and enable sliding \citep{cuffey2010physics,tulaczyk2000basal,boulton2001sediment}. 
%Inferred friction can also depend on assumptions about ice rheology and subglacial hydrology \citep{rathmann2022inferred,mcarthur2023basal}.
Gravity disturbances reflect subsurface density variations associated with bedrock and sedimentary structure \citep{hackney2003geodetic}. Gravity data can therefore constrain broad bed geometry where direct measurements are sparse \citep{an2017bed}. Bedrock lithology can influence sediment characteristics, while deformable sediment can reduce basal resistance \citep{crompton2016correlations,boulton2001sediment}. Gravity measurements cannot resolve the thin (centimeter- to meter-scale) sediment layers that affect sliding, but larger density contrasts can identify sedimentary basins; such sediment bodies can influence ice-stream onset \citep{bell1998influence,studinger2001subglacial,peters2006subglacial}. Magnetic anomalies reflect contrasts in crustal magnetization \citep{golynsky2018new}. Together with gravity and radar data, they can help infer subglacial geology \citep{jordan2023geological} and regional thermal structure \citep{martos2017heat,reading2022antarctic}, providing indirect context for basal drag. Geothermal heat flux supplies heat at the bed and can influence whether basal ice reaches the pressure-melting point. Where basal ice is at the melting point, additional heat can support basal meltwater production and thereby influence basal resistance \citep{dawson2022ice,kang2021evaluation}.

Previous studies have compared inferred basal resistance with radar, seismic, and other observations of the subglacial environment, but have not established whether it follows a consistent local relationship with these observations. \citet{haris2024can} found that regions with distinct radar signatures differ in mean basal shear stress, but they compared distributions across regions rather than testing a relationship at individual locations.
\citet{das2023quest} found no robust correlation between inferred sliding
parameters and radar-derived basal reflectivity over Thwaites Glacier, while
\citet{kyrke2017can} found no significant kilometer-scale correlation between basal
drag and seismic acoustic impedance along Pine Island Glacier (PIG), although
values averaged along each seismic profile correlated more strongly.

These studies, together with our understanding of basal processes, suggest that the relationship between basal conditions and basal resistance may be complex and nonlinear, involving thermo-mechanical feedbacks, subglacial hydrology, and spatially variable bed properties. Following the field inversion and machine learning approach of \citet{parish2016paradigm}, we use nonlinear regression with a multilayer perceptron (MLP) as a flexible representation of $f$ in Eqn~\ref{eq:functional_relationship} to relate the selected observables to the inversion-derived friction control $C$.

 The point-wise architecture asks whether the selected observables at a location contain enough information to determine the inversion control there, without prescribing a particular functional form. We deliberately exclude observed velocity from the predictor set because changing $C$ changes the modeled velocity; including velocity as an input would introduce a self-consistency problem (in preliminary tests, iterating $C$ and velocity to agreement did not converge) and would make the inferred relationship depend directly on the velocity used to evaluate it.

Recent machine-learning studies in glaciology have addressed related but distinct inverse and constitutive problems. \citet{jouvet2023inversion} inverted a convolutional emulator of Stokes flow, while \citet{jouvet2023ice} and \citet{cheng2024forward} incorporated ice-flow physics into learned forward or inverse models. \citet{wang2025deep} used remote sensing and physics-informed deep learning to infer spatially varying ice-shelf rheology. These approaches use spatial structure or governing equations to reproduce ice-flow fields or solve inverse problems. In contrast, the present study uses machine learning only as a flexible function approximator for $f(\cdot)$ in Eqn~\ref{eq:functional_relationship} to address a different question: do commonly available ice and subglacial observables contain enough information to define a point-wise relationship with inversion-derived basal friction that holds across locations?

\subsection{Study region and observations}

The study domain covers the Pine Island, Thwaites, and Dotson--Crosson drainage systems in the Amundsen Sea sector of West Antarctica (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_region}). The catchment outlines were delineated manually, with lateral boundaries following glacier velocity flowlines. PIG, Thwaites, and Dotson are geographic labels; they do not imply that each catchment contains one basal regime.

We take the observed velocity from the Making Earth System Data Records for Use in Research Environments (MEaSUREs) Phase-Based Antarctica Ice Velocity Map, derived from satellite radar interferometry \citep{https://doi.org/10.5067/pz3nj5rxrh10}. Surface elevation, ice thickness, and bed elevation are taken from BedMachine Antarctica, version~2 \citep{https://doi.org/10.5067/e1ql9hfq7a8m,morlighem2020bedmachine}. BedMachine combines direct observations with reconstruction that uses ice velocity in some regions. The ice-product predictors, and the bed-derived subglacial-product predictors, therefore
represent available geometry products, some of which already incorporate
velocity information, although velocity itself is not used as a predictor. BedMachine uses different methods to reconstruct ice thickness between
observations. Most of our test regions (detailed in the following sections) lie in regions mapped
using streamline-diffusion interpolation, which favors smoother
thickness variations along flow than across flow
\citep{morlighem2020bedmachine}.

Surface air temperature and geothermal heat flux follow the products distributed with Quantarctica \citep{matsuoka2018quantarctica,comiso2000variability,an2015temperature}. The magnetic anomaly is taken from the 5~km EPSG:3031 ADMAP-2S grid \citep{eagles2024admap}. \subsection{Ice-flow model and basal-friction inversion}

All simulations used Icepack \citep{shapero2021icepack} and a depth-integrated stress balance based on the shallow-shelf approximation \citep{macayeal1989large}. The unstructured triangular mesh has 9,069 vertices and 17,653 elements over 310,546~km$^2$, with a median element edge length of 6.6~km. The basal-friction control uses continuous quadratic elements on this mesh, giving 35,797 control points. The shallow-shelf approximation neglects vertical shear, so the modeled horizontal velocity is compared directly with the observed surface velocity. The model parameter $C_b$ is model-dependent rather than a unique representation of basal conditions as the inferred basal-friction control depends on the adopted stress-balance approximation and basal law.

Basal friction is represented with a Weertman-type law,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weertman}
\boldsymbol{\tau}_b=-C_b\,\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^{1/m-1}\mathbf{u},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\tau}_b$ is the basal shear stress on the ice, $\mathbf{u}$ is depth-averaged horizontal velocity, $m=3$ is the sliding exponent, and $C_b$ is a dimensional basal-friction coefficient with units MPa~(m~a$^{-1}$)$^{-1/3}$. A Weertman law is a simplified hard-bed parameterization and does not explicitly represent processes such as effective-pressure dependence, cavitation, or deforming till. We hold the basal law fixed so that the experiment tests the proposed mapping with one consistent ice-flow model.

We prescribe observed velocities along the non-terminus boundaries and apply an ocean-pressure traction condition at the termini. Positivity and the grounding transition are imposed through
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:friction_parameterization}
C_b=\phi C_0 e^{C},
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the dimensionless inversion control used to represent spatial variation in basal friction and $C_0=0.01$~MPa~(m~a$^{-1}$)$^{-1/3}$. $\phi$ is a height-above-flotation ramp that continuously reduces basal resistance as flotation is approached and enforces $C_b=0$ over floating ice \citep{leguy2014parameterization,seroussi2014hydrostatic}. Appendix section~\nameref{app:ssa} describes the full stress balance and grounding transition. 

%Thus, the MLP predicts $C$, while the dimensional coefficient entering the stress balance is $C_b$.

The inversion minimizes the total objective function
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:objective}
J(\mathbf{u},C;r_C)=\frac{1}{2NU^2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_i)-\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathbf{x}_i)\right\rVert^2+
\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\left(\frac{L}{r_C}\right)^2\int_{\Omega}\lVert\nabla C\rVert^2\,\mathrm{d}A,
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of paired-velocity observations, $|\Omega|$ is the domain area, $U=1$~m~a$^{-1}$ and $L=7.5$~km are fixed reference scales that make both terms dimensionless, and $r_C$ controls smoothness. Smaller $r_C$ gives stronger smoothing. The misfit is unweighted, matching the velocity root-mean-square error (RMSE) that we use for evaluation. Following the use of L-curves in ice-sheet inverse models
\citep{wolovick2023regularization}, we selected
$r_C \simeq 0.014$ using a maximum-curvature criterion
(Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:lcurve}). We obtain gradients of Eqn~\ref{eq:objective} using the adjoint method. Only $C$ is inverted; the rate factor in Glen's flow law is prescribed from the englacial temperature field of \citet{van2013using}. We use the resulting inversion friction control field (Fig.~\ref{fig:definitive_inversion}b) for every learning experiment. 

%Following the use of L-curves in ice-sheet inverse models \citep{wolovick2023regularization}, a maximum-curvature criterion selected $r_C\simeq0.014$ from the refined candidates. The adjacent candidate $r_C\simeq0.017$ had curvature within approximately 4\% of the selected value, so the curve identifies a narrow elbow range rather than one exact value. The sector-wide inversion began from exactly $C=0$ and reached a stable endpoint after 300 optimization iterations.

The one additional predictor in CFG06 relative to CFG05 measures bed--surface gradient alignment,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alignment}
a_{bs}=
\frac{\nabla b\cdot\nabla s}
{\sqrt{\left(\lVert\nabla b\rVert^2+\epsilon_b^2\right)
\left(\lVert\nabla s\rVert^2+\epsilon_s^2\right)}},

\subsection{Spatially withheld square experiments and predictor support}

The primary experiment uses ten 50~km squares distributed across the study
region (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_region}b). For each square, we exclude the full 130~km footprint from the training and validation datasets, leaving a 40~km buffer on each side of the central evaluation square. The ten withheld regions do
not overlap. Following the rationale of spatially separated evaluation \citep{roberts2017crossvalidation,valavi2019blockcv}, the buffer reduces dependence on nearby spatially correlated training rows. It does not remove the
dependence introduced by the common sector-wide inversion or by non-local ice
flow. The central 50~km square is the primary evaluation area; the surrounding
buffer and complete footprint are used only for maps and secondary summaries,
not as additional independent tests. We chose the 40~km buffer before model fitting from the spatial correlation of observed ice speed, which falls to about 0.1 at 40~km separation (Appendix section~\nameref{app:buffer}).

We selected candidate square locations using spatial separation and predictor-support criteria so that the remaining training region generally represented the withheld predictor values well. We assessed support using both marginal and joint criteria. Marginal support requires every included predictor at a held-out point to lie
within the 1st--99th percentile range of the corresponding eligible non-held-out
rows. Joint support asks whether the complete predictor combination has a
sufficiently close analog in the remaining training region. We report the area fraction satisfying both criteria; support is not used to select which points are evaluated. Details of the joint-support
transformation and distance threshold are given in Appendix section~\nameref{app:predictor_population}. A separate regional experiment withholds the two inter-catchment corridors and is reported in Appendix section~\nameref{app:target_distributions}.

We used no criterion based on the inversion-reference $C$ or modeled velocity errors to select squares. We examined the distribution of inversion-reference $C$ only afterward because the reference field is a regularized, non-unique inversion solution, and a different predicted $C$ may still reproduce the observed velocity. We did not use the inversion-reference $C$ or modeled velocity error to select the squares, so their locations do not depend on how well the predicted $C$ performs there.

\subsubsection{Catchment-scale transfer test}

In addition to the ten spatial tests, we use PIG as a catchment-scale transfer test chosen before we examined its results. This tests whether an MLP can transfer a point-wise relationship learned elsewhere in the Amundsen Sea sector across an entire glacier catchment. We excluded all eligible PIG rows from the training and validation dataset; the remaining grounded sector, including Thwaites, Dotson--Crosson, and the two inter-catchment corridors, provided the training and validation rows. We evaluated predictor support in PIG using the same marginal and joint criteria as the square experiments, without using $C$ or velocity errors. 

\subsection{Model fitting and repeated realizations}

We use an MLP as a flexible nonlinear representation of $f(\cdot)$ in Eqn~\ref{eq:functional_relationship} to test whether inversion-derived friction can be expressed as a local function of the available predictors. An MLP is a neural network in which each layer forms weighted sums of its inputs and passes them through a nonlinear function; stacking several layers lets it represent nonlinear relationships without a prescribed form. The model uses only the predictor values at each location. Approaches that also use neighboring values, such as convolutional or graph neural networks, would test a different hypothesis: that $C$ depends on the spatial pattern of the predictors around a location, and not only on their local values. The MLP contains ten hidden layers of 200 units and predicts the reference \(C\) using the predictors in each configuration. We robustly scale inputs and targets using medians and interquartile ranges calculated from the training data only. Appendix section~\nameref{app:training_campaign} provides further details on the network implementation, training procedure, repeated realizations, and training and validation histories.

For each square experiment, we reproducibly split the eligible rows remaining after removing the excluded footprint into 90\% for training and 10\% for validation. Nearby training and validation rows are not statistically
independent because of interpolation, predictor resolution, spatial
autocorrelation, and regularization of the inversion-reference field. We therefore use the random split only for model fitting and selection, and evaluate performance separately in the spatially withheld central square. Appendix section~\nameref{app:predictor_population} provides exact row counts.

For each experiment and predictor configuration, we train ten reproducible
realizations that differ in the training/validation split, initial weights, and
epoch shuffling. Using multiple realizations reduces sensitivity to any one
set of training choices and provides a more stable estimate of the predicted
$C$ field \citep{hansen1990neural,breiman1996bagging}. Each realization uses the same training and validation rows in every predictor configuration, so the configurations can be compared directly. The differences among the ten realizations show how much the predicted $C$ depends on these training choices; they are not an estimate of prediction uncertainty. The prediction for each experiment and configuration is the median of the ten predicted $C$ fields at each mesh control point.

\subsection{Forward simulations and measures of performance}

We evaluate each median predicted $C$ field in a separate Icepack forward simulation. For each square, predicted $C$ replaces the inversion-reference $C$ only in the grounded, eligible part of the 130~km withheld region. The inversion-reference $C$ is retained
everywhere outside that footprint. The PIG experiment
uses the same procedure over the withheld catchment.
Appendix section~\nameref{app:replacement_sensitivity} compares this design with replacing $C$ across the whole sector.
At the edge of the replaced region, the control changes steeply but
continuously to the inversion-reference $C$ within the mesh cells that cross
the edge; we applied no additional smoothing. All forward simulations
converged within 50 Newton iterations.
%This controlled evaluation comprises 60 square simulations using predicted $C$, one PIG simulation using predicted $C$, ten square-specific uniform-$C$ simulations, and one PIG uniform-$C$ simulation.

The primary performance measure is vector velocity RMSE (hereafter velocity RMSE) against the original paired MEaSUREs velocity components at their regular 450~m pixel centers over the evaluation area $A$. We evaluate modeled velocity at the same locations. The inversion misfit (Eqn~\ref{eq:objective}) is computed at these same pixel centers. This evaluation is distinct from the mesh-interpolated observation field that the solver uses for boundary conditions and as its initial velocity. The spatial average below is approximated using the mean squared vector residual at these equally spaced points:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:vector_rmse}
\mathrm{RMSE}_{u}=
\left[
\frac{
\int_A
\lVert\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{ML}}-\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{obs}}\rVert^2
\,\mathrm{d}A
}{
\int_A \mathrm{d}A
}
\right]^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
The subscript ML denotes the simulation using the MLP-predicted $C$.

For the maps, we also use the local vector-error magnitude
\[
e(\mathbf{x}) =
\left\|\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{u}_{\mathrm{obs}}(\mathbf{x})\right\|.
\]

The spatially uniform-$C$ baseline uses a single control value with no spatial variation from the predictors. This value is the finite-element-area-weighted mean inversion-reference $C$ over the grounded cells ($\phi>0.1$) of the training and validation region and is applied uniformly within the withheld region. Because $C_b\propto\exp(C)$, averaging $C$ corresponds to a
geometric mean of $C_0\exp(C)$ before multiplication by the grounding ramp
$\phi$. We apply it over the same withheld replacement region as the MLP-predicted field, retaining the inversion-reference $C$ outside that region in both simulations, so the two simulations differ only in the spatial pattern of $C$ inside that region.
The comparison therefore asks whether spatial variation in $C$ inferred from the predictors improves modeled velocity beyond what a single representative friction control provides. We also report $\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{relative}} = \mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{ML}}/\mathrm{RMSE}_{\mathrm{uniform}}$; values below
one indicate improvement over the uniform-$C$ baseline.

The ten held-out squares are the independent spatial tests. For each predictor configuration, we summarize performance by the median and interquartile range of the ten square RMSE values. We also compare configurations that differ only by added predictors square by square, counting how many squares each performs better in.

Predictor support indicates whether similar predictor combinations are represented in the training region, but does not distinguish between densely and sparsely sampled parts of predictor space. We therefore examine whether predictor combinations with fewer similar training rows, that is, lower training density, are associated with errors in the predicted $C$ for CFG02 and CFG04 (Appendix section~\nameref{app:training_representation}). We measure training density by the distance from each held-out row to its 20th-nearest training row in predictor space, expressed as a percentile $p$ of the same distance among training rows, so that a high $p$ means few similar training rows. 15.9~m~a$^{-1}$, compared with 12.3~m~a$^{-1}$ for the inversion-reference

persist across the complete withheld footprint despite high
predictor support. The selected predictors therefore contain information that can improve modeled velocity relative to a uniform control, but they do not define a point-wise relationship that is consistently successful across locations.

in fast flow. The fastest 10\% of the evaluated area, corresponding to observed speeds
above approximately $504\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$, contributes 59.8\% of the
total squared-error reduction relative to uniform $C$, but still accounts
for 89.5\% of the CFG02 squared error. In the fastest flow
($>1000\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$), the RMSE remains
$1390.0\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$, compared with
$1448.8\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$ for uniform $C$. Thus, CFG02 improves much of
the slow and intermediate-speed catchment interior and parts of the
fast-flowing trunk, but provides only a modest relative improvement in the
fastest downstream flow. This spatial concentration explains how CFG02 can
lower error across most of the catchment while retaining a large
catchment-wide RMSE.

Locations where the inversion-reference error is at least $100\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$ comprise 3.9\% of the evaluated PIG area but contain 47.1\% of the CFG02 squared error. The remaining error is not confined to those locations: most locations with CFG02 error above $100\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$ lie outside regions where the inversion-reference error exceeds $100\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$. Predictor support alone does not explain the spatial pattern. For CFG02,
marginal support covers 97.5\% of the evaluated PIG area, joint support covers
93.3\%, and 91.9\% satisfies both criteria. Of the net squared-error reduction
relative to uniform $C$, 79.8\% occurs within the area satisfying both support
criteria. CFG02 nevertheless contains both successful and unsuccessful regions
where predictor support is strong.
CFG02 has a $C$ RMSE of 0.85 across PIG. Locations with larger absolute differences from reference $C$ also tend to have larger velocity errors, with a Spearman correlation of 0.67. This indicates a relatively strong positive association and is larger than the median within-square correlation of 0.32 for CFG02. The association between the training-density percentile $p$ and absolute $C$ error is much weaker. $C$ RMSE increases from 0.742 to 0.877 and 0.898 across the three categories of progressively lower training density, but the Spearman correlation between $p$ and absolute $C$ error is only 0.031. This near-zero correlation indicates that fewer nearby training examples do not consistently correspond to higher local $C$ error across PIG.



\subsection{Implications for the point-wise hypothesis}

Taken together, the experiments address three related questions: whether the
predictor values in a held-out region are represented in the training data,
whether the predicted $C$ resembles the reference $C$, and whether the
predicted $C$ improves modeled velocity. The results show that these questions
can have different answers. High predictor support can coexist with poor
velocity performance, and lower training density does not consistently
produce larger $C$ errors. Agreement with the reference $C$ is also related to,
but does not determine, the resulting velocity error: CFG05, for example, has a median $R_C^2$ of $-1.41$ in the central squares yet improves on uniform $C$ in nine of ten. Nor can the predictors,
taken together, indicate in advance where the predicted $C$ will improve on
uniform $C$: a classifier trained on all twelve predictors is rarely better than
predicting whichever outcome is more common in the withheld region (Appendix~\nameref{app:transfer_predictability}). The PIG experiment shows
that the same distinctions remain important when the held-out region is
expanded from a 50~km square to an entire glacier catchment.

The contrast between random and spatial holdouts bears directly on the
hypothesis in Eqn~\ref{eq:functional_relationship}. If $C$ were a point-wise
function of the predictors, a relationship that reproduces randomly withheld
rows should reproduce spatially withheld rows with comparable accuracy wherever
similar predictor values are well represented in the training data. CFG02
reproduces randomly withheld validation rows with a median $C$ RMSE of 0.08 and
$R_C^2$ of 0.99 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:validation_c_diagnostics}), but in the withheld
central squares its median $C$ RMSE is 0.30 and $R_C^2$ is negative in eight of
ten (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:c_diagnostics}), although 94--99\% of every square satisfies
both support criteria (Fig.~\ref{fig:support_skill}a). A fitted model can
transfer poorly for other reasons, such as limited sampling of the predictor
space or approximation error, but the errors do not decrease where the training
data are denser: held-out rows whose training density is at least as high as
that of a typical training row have the same median $C$ RMSE of
0.30 (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:representation_c}). The difference is therefore not
explained by the support and training-density measures tested here, and it points
to structure in the inversion-derived $C$ that the local predictors do not
capture. Selecting the regularization or the stopping point against spatially
withheld data might improve transfer, but a gain obtained by shrinking the
prediction toward a spatially uniform control would reflect less use of the
predictors rather than a closer point-wise relationship with them.

The spatial differences in performance are not simply explained by the
BedMachine diagnostics either. Reported bed-elevation uncertainty has
only a weak relationship with local velocity error across the square tests
(Appendix~\nameref{app:bedmachine_diagnostics}). Comparisons among BedMachine
source classes are more limited because eight of the ten central squares
contain only streamline-diffusion cells. Nevertheless, CFG02 velocity RMSE
ranges from 3.5 to 136.3~m~a$^{-1}$ across those eight squares, showing that
differences in reconstruction class alone cannot explain the large spatial
variation in performance. Reconstruction may still matter within a source class; for example, smoothing may remove local bed variations that could help distinguish basal settings, but the present analysis does not isolate this effect.

More fundamentally, the available predictors may not capture all of the
physical variability relevant to $C$. Small-scale basal roughness can
influence basal resistance \citep{hoffman2022impact}. The observed velocity reflects
that influence, so the inverted $C$ contains it, but none of the predictors resolves
roughness at these scales. Spatial variation in $C$ caused by unresolved roughness
therefore cannot be predicted from the available observables.
The heat-flux, gravity, and magnetic products also provide indirect information at relatively
coarse spatial resolution. Errors in the predictors can also hide a relationship that
does exist. Where a predictor is poorly known, locations with the same bed
conditions can have different predictor values, and locations with different
conditions can have similar ones, so even a real dependence of $C$ on that
property would be blurred and difficult to learn. Our tests therefore concern
the predictors as currently observed, not the bed properties they represent. In addition, the inferred $C$ depends on the
adopted stress balance, basal law, prescribed ice rheology, and regularized
inversion, rather than representing a unique physical property of the bed.
The ice softness is prescribed rather than inverted, so $C$ also absorbs any error in it.

The point-wise formulation itself may also be limiting. The MLP uses only the
predictor values at each location and cannot use information from neighboring
locations. At the same time, membrane stresses couple the velocity response
across space, so changes in $C$ at one location can affect velocity elsewhere.
This is consistent with theoretical limits on local basal inference, in which
basal disturbances are filtered through spatially coupled ice flow before
appearing at the surface
\citep{gudmundsson2003transmission,raymond2005relationship,
gudmundsson2008limit}. The present experiments therefore identify several
possible explanations for the remaining errors, but do not determine which
mechanism is responsible in any individual region.

\section{Conclusion}

This study tested whether inversion-derived basal-friction control $C$ can be represented by a point-wise relationship with commonly available ice and subglacial observables. We evaluated this relationship in ten spatially held-out regions across the Amundsen Sea sector and in a separate PIG catchment test by inserting the predicted $C$ fields into Icepack and comparing the resulting velocities with observations.

The tested local observables contain information that can produce $C$ fields that improve modeled velocity relative to uniform $C$ in held-out regions, but that relationship is not spatially consistent across the Amundsen Sea sector. The ice-product configurations give the lowest median velocity errors, and the combined configurations improve on a spatially uniform-$C$ baseline in nine of ten squares. However, performance varies strongly across locations; adding predictors does not systematically improve performance, and poor performance can occur even when the training data represent the held-out predictor values well. Training density provides no simple explanation either: lower training density does not consistently correspond to larger $C$ errors across the square tests. Withholding all of PIG shows the same limitation at catchment scale, with CFG02 reducing velocity RMSE by only about 8\% relative to uniform $C$. Although CFG02 lowers local velocity error over most of PIG, the remaining error is strongly concentrated in fast flow. The spatially withheld squares show that high predictor support can coexist with poor velocity performance. Agreement with the reference $C$ is also only partly related to velocity performance: local $C$ error and velocity error are positively associated, but not one-to-one, and over a region, closer overall agreement with $C$ does not translate proportionally into lower velocity RMSE.

Under the framework considered here, the Amundsen Sea sector, the selected data products, a point-wise MLP, and a shallow-shelf/Weertman inversion, the tested ice and subglacial observables yield fitted controls that improve modeled velocity in most test regions, but they do not support a single point-wise relationship with inversion-derived $C$ that is consistently successful across locations. More broadly, the results show that such parameterizations should be tested in spatially held-out regions and evaluated through modeled velocity, not only through agreement with reference $C$. The same framework can be used to test other predictors and methods that use information from neighboring locations.

\section{Ice-flow equations, grounding transition, and inversion method}
\label{app:ssa}

Icepack solves the depth-integrated shallow-shelf stress balance
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ssa_tensor}
\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(h\mathbf{M}\right)+\boldsymbol{\tau}_b
=\rho_i g h\nabla s,
\end{equation}
where $h$ is ice thickness, $s$ is surface elevation, $\rho_i$ is ice
density, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $\boldsymbol{\tau}_b$ is
the basal shear stress acting on the ice. For horizontal depth-averaged velocity $\mathbf{u}$, the membrane-stress tensor used here is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:membrane_stress}
\mathbf{M}=2\mu\left[\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
+\mathrm{tr}(\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})\mathbf{I}\right],\qquad
\mu=\frac{1}{2}A^{-1/n}\dot{\epsilon}_e^{1/n-1},
\end{equation}
with $\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}= (\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm T})/2$, $\dot{\epsilon}_e=\left[(\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}: \dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}+\mathrm{tr}(\dot{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})^2 +\dot{\epsilon}_{\min}^2)/2\right]^{1/2}$, $\mathbf{I}$ the horizontal identity tensor, $\dot{\epsilon}_{\min}=10^{-5}$~a$^{-1}$ Icepack's default strain-rate floor, $A$ the temperature-dependent rate factor, and $n=3$ the Glen exponent. The modeled velocity is depth-averaged. Its comparison with the MEaSUREs surface velocity therefore uses the shallow-shelf assumption that vertical shear is negligible. We use the depth-averaged englacial temperature of \citet{van2013using} and linearly interpolate this field to the model mesh and supply it in kelvin to Icepack's rate-factor function to calculate $A$ \citep{shapero2021icepack}. This differs from the surface-air-temperature predictor used by the MLP.

The basal law is Eqn~\ref{eq:weertman}, with the Icepack sliding exponent $m=3$ in that equation. The dimensionless basal-friction control $C$ is distinct from the dimensional basal-friction coefficient $C_b$ that enters the friction law given in equation \ref{eq:phi}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:phi}
\begin{split}
p_w&=\rho_w g\max(0,h-s),\qquad p_i=\rho_i g h,\\
\phi&=
\begin{cases}
0, & p_i=0,\\
\max\!\left[1-\left(p_w/p_i\right)^p,0\right], & p_i>0,
\end{cases}\\
C_b&=\phi C_0\exp(C).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here $\rho_w$ is seawater density, $p_w$ is the hydrostatic water-pressure
estimate, $p_i$ is ice overburden pressure, $p=1$ is the ramp exponent used
in this study, and $C_0=0.01$~MPa~(m~a$^{-1}$)$^{-1/3}$. Consequently $C_b$ has
units MPa~(m~a$^{-1}$)$^{-1/3}$, while $C$ and $\phi$ are dimensionless.
The ramp is continuous and piecewise, with abrupt changes in slope introduced by the maximum
operations.

Quadratic finite-element basis functions are used. We prescribe observed velocities along the non-terminus boundaries and apply an ocean-pressure traction condition at the termini. In that terminus condition the water-depth magnitude is $d=\max(h-s,0)$ and the normal-stress contribution is proportional to $(\rho_i g h^2-\rho_w g d^2)/2$. At 11 edge points without a valid MEaSUREs value, we used the nearest valid pixel.

We initialized the inversion with $C=0$ and ran it for up to 300 optimization iterations. It converged after 257 iterations, when the gradient norm was $6.7\times10^{-5}$, below the $10^{-3}$ acceptance threshold, and the misfit and roughness had stopped changing (relative changes below $4\times10^{-5}$ over the final iterations). Figure~\ref{fig:lcurve} shows the L-curve used to select the
regularization parameter. The maximum-curvature criterion selected
$r_C\simeq0.014$ from the refined candidates. The adjacent candidate,
$r_C\simeq0.017$, had curvature within approximately 7\% of the selected
value, indicating a narrow elbow range rather than a uniquely resolved
regularization value. We also tried values of $r_C$ outside this
range. With weaker smoothing, $r_C=0.05$ did not meet the convergence criteria within the iteration limit, and $r_C=0.1$ failed numerically.

\section{Spatial separation and buffer choice}
\label{app:buffer}

For each 50~km evaluation square, we exclude an additional 40~km buffer
from MLP training and validation on all sides, giving a 130~km excluded
footprint. The buffer is intended to reduce the influence of nearby,
spatially correlated training data on the withheld-square test. We chose the
40~km distance before model fitting using the spatial scale of observed
ice-speed variations.

Using MEaSUREs speed $v$, we formed
\begin{equation}
q(\mathbf{x})=\ln\!\left[1+v(\mathbf{x})/v_0\right],\qquad
v_0=1~\mathrm{m~a^{-1}}.
\end{equation}
The logarithm reduces the influence of the fastest outlets, while the offset
keeps the transformation finite where speed approaches zero. We then removed
the broad sector-scale variation using a normalized Gaussian average with a
50~km standard deviation, leaving local variations in log speed. These local
variations have an estimated $1/e$ correlation length of 22.6~km, and their
correlation falls to 0.108 at a separation of 40~km. Varying the Gaussian
scale from 30 to 100~km yields $1/e$ correlation lengths of
16.1--46.0~km.

Fast-flow widths that we measured from the MEaSUREs speed map \citep{https://doi.org/10.5067/pz3nj5rxrh10} have a median of 10.5--18~km and a 90th percentile of 36--38~km across speed thresholds of 100, 250, and 500~m~a$^{-1}$, so the 50~km test side exceeds the width of most fast-flowing tributaries in the sector and spans ten cells of the coarsest 5~km predictors. Increasing the buffer from 40 to 50~km would reduce the local log-speed-anomaly correlation only from 0.108 to 0.072 while enlarging each excluded footprint from 16,900 to 22,500~km$^2$, a 33\% increase. We therefore selected 40~km to balance separation from training rows against the loss of training and validation data. Observed speed is used here as a proxy for the spatial scale of the flow, not of $C$ itself. The buffer does not isolate the withheld square physically: the sector-wide inversion and the non-local membrane-stress response still couple it to the surrounding ice.

\section{Eligible rows, predictor construction, and support}
\label{app:predictor_population}

This section defines the common set of observation-grid rows used across all
predictor configurations, describes how the derived predictors are constructed,
and explains how predictor support is evaluated in the held-out regions. Of the MEaSUREs pixels with valid values of both velocity components, 1,622,598 are located in the model mesh by Firedrake's point-location routine. All twelve predictors
are finite at those rows. Applying $\phi>0.1$ leaves 1,530,992
common eligible rows, which are used by every predictor configuration. Figure~\ref{fig:eligible_region_map} maps this eligible region against the complete modeled domain. 


For the ten square experiments, excluding the 130~km footprint leaves 1,447,471--1,463,861 rows available for training and validation. Each MLP's reproducible 90/10 split contains 1,302,723--1,317,474 training rows and 144,748--146,387 validation rows. The central-square evaluation sets contain 12,321 rows, except SQ03, which contains 12,432. For each training realization, every predictor configuration uses the same training locations and the same validation locations. Rows outside the common grounded eligible area retain inversion-reference $C$ in every forward simulation. White areas in maps lie outside the region shown for that experiment or outside the common mask; no locations were removed based on target values or model performance.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.60\linewidth]{figures/appendix/figure_appendix_eligible_region_map.pdf}
\caption{The modeled domain and the eligible region used for training and for every reported metric. The excluded area (orange, 5.4\% of the modeled domain) is where $\phi\leq0.1$ or no valid MEaSUREs velocity is available. It is simulated but never scored; observations on floating ice still enter the inversion misfit. Every other map in the paper shows only the eligible region.}

Surface elevation $s$, thickness $h$, and bed elevation $b$ are represented
as finite-element scalar fields. Their gradients are evaluated as
finite-element vector expressions, interpolated component-wise to the
observation grid, and summarized as
$\lVert\nabla f\rVert=(f_x^2+f_y^2)^{1/2}$ for
$f\in\{s,h,b\}$. Driving-stress magnitude is
\begin{equation}
\tau_d=\rho_i g h\lVert\nabla s\rVert,
\end{equation}
reported in the stress unit used by Icepack, MPa.

Eqn~\ref{eq:alignment} gives the alignment predictor. The small-gradient
scales were estimated by dividing representative elevation-error scales by the
median mesh-edge length of 6553.4~m. For the surface, a 1~m elevation-error
scale based on the reported accuracy of the Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica \citep{howat2019reference} gives
$\epsilon_s=1.5\times10^{-4}$. For the bed, the median BedMachine
bed-elevation error over grounded mesh points is 35.31~m, giving
$\epsilon_b=5.4\times10^{-3}$. We rounded and fixed the resulting values before evaluating the model. We clip the alignment value to $[-1,1]$. The $\epsilon_b$ and $\epsilon_s$
terms in the denominator reduce the magnitude of the alignment toward zero when either gradient is small relative to its corresponding scale. Table~\ref{tab:predictor_provenance} summarizes the native spatial resolution and interpretation of the products used.


We evaluate marginal support separately for each predictor. A held-out point satisfies the marginal criterion when every predictor included in that configuration lies within the 1st--99th percentile range of the corresponding eligible non-held-out rows. Joint support evaluates the full combination of predictor values. We first sample the predictors on a 5~km grid covering the sector. A 5~km grid is used rather than the 450~m MEaSUREs grid because the predictors are finite-element fields on a mesh with a median edge length of 6.6~km and the coarsest products have 5~km spacing, so finer sampling would mainly add near-duplicate values; the grid also allowed the squares to be selected before the 450~m dataset was built. We transform each predictor to normal scores, replacing every value by the standard-normal quantile of its rank \citep{beasley2009rank}, so that all predictors share a common, unitless scale. We then use principal component analysis \citep{jolliffe2016principal} to reduce the predictor space while retaining at least 99\% of the variance of the transformed predictors, and scale the retained components to unit variance. Each location is then a point in this transformed space, and the Euclidean distance between two points measures how different their predictor combinations are, with every retained component weighted equally. We use the same transformation for all spatial experiments.

A held-out point is jointly supported if some training location has a sufficiently similar predictor combination, that is, lies close to it in this space. To define how close is sufficient, we use the distances found within the sector itself. For each grid point, we
calculate the distance in transformed predictor space to the most similar point
located at least 40~km away along at least one grid axis, so that the excluded
neighborhood is a square, matching the square shape of the 40~km buffer. The 95th percentile of these distances defines the
joint-support threshold. For each held-out
point, we then find the most similar 5~km reference-grid point within the corresponding training region. The held-out point satisfies the joint criterion when this distance does not exceed the threshold. This construction follows the area-of-applicability approach of \citet{meyer2021predicting}, which measures the distance from each prediction location to the most similar training location in a normalized predictor space.

We use the same construction twice: to select the squares, and to report support for each configuration. Square selection used all twelve predictors, so a candidate square had to be supported in the full predictor space: at least 95\% of its area had to lie within the marginal bounds of each predictor, and at least 95\% had to satisfy the joint criterion. The support values reported for each configuration use only that configuration's predictors, transformation and threshold, so passing the selection check does not guarantee the same support for every configuration: a square selected with all twelve predictors can have lower support for an individual configuration (Fig.~\ref{fig:support_skill}a). Reported predictor support is the area fraction satisfying both the marginal and joint criteria. We retain all held-out points in the modeled velocity evaluation, regardless of support.

\subsection{Training density and prediction error}
\label{app:training_representation}

Predictor support shows whether similar predictor combinations occur in the training data, but not how many there are. Here we test whether prediction error depends on that number, which we call the training density. For CFG02 and CFG04 in the ten central squares, and CFG02 in PIG, we measure the training density at each held-out row with $d_{20}$, the distance to its 20th-most-similar training row. We measure this distance in the transformed predictor space used for joint support, search all training rows, and apply no geographic separation. A small $d_{20}$ means that many similar training rows exist; a large $d_{20}$ means that few do. This is the standard nearest-neighbor measure of data density \citep{loftsgaarden1965nonparametric}.

To judge whether a held-out $d_{20}$ is small or large, we compare it with the $d_{20}$ of 10,000 randomly chosen training rows, calculated in the same way. Its percentile $p$ is the percentage of these training values that are smaller (ties count as half). We calculate $p$ separately for each of the ten MLPs, whose training rows differ slightly, and use the median. A high $p$ means that the held-out row has fewer similar training rows than a typical training row has. We group the rows into three categories, $p\leq50$, $50<p\leq95$, and $p>95$, which we fixed, together with the neighbor count, before examining prediction errors. We do not rebalance the training data: the predictors are continuous and partly related, so balancing them one at a time would not balance their combinations. To test whether the neighbor count or training rows close by on the map affect the result, we repeat the analysis using the 100th and 500th most similar training rows, and using only training rows at least 40~km away.

Within each category, we calculate $C$ RMSE between the median predicted $C$ and the reference $C$ at the held-out rows, and velocity RMSE and relative RMSE over the same rows. The result also does not depend on the neighbor count or on nearby training rows: using the 100th or 500th most similar training row, or only training rows at least 40~km away, gives median within-square correlations between $-0.13$ and 0.09.

Relative velocity RMSE does not follow training density either. In SQ06, CFG02 is worse than uniform $C$ in the highest-density category (relative RMSE 1.15) but better in the lowest (0.96). In SQ05, its relative velocity RMSE rises from 0.37 in the highest-density category to 0.81 in the lowest, whereas for CFG04 in SQ02 it falls from 1.45 to 1.34. Lower training density therefore does not consistently mean larger velocity error.

\section{MLP training}
\label{app:training_campaign}

This section describes the MLP architecture and training procedure and the ten training runs for each experiment.

The MLP has ten hidden layers of 200 units. Each hidden layer is followed by batch normalization and a sigmoid linear unit (SiLU) activation, and the final layer is linear. Predictors and $C$ are robustly scaled using medians and interquartile ranges fitted on training rows only. Adam uses an initial learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and batch size 1024, with reproducible epoch-wise shuffling. The learning rate is multiplied by 0.2, down to $10^{-6}$, after 15 epochs without a decrease in validation mean squared error (MSE) of at least $10^{-4}$. Training stops after 45 epochs without such a decrease, and we keep the weights from the epoch with the lowest validation MSE.

A separate CFG06 calculation selected the L2 penalty without using any of the ten central squares. It retained the square buffers, PIG and the inter-catchment corridors, so the regional tests were withheld from their final MLP fits but not from this calibration. Its fixed 70/20/10 split contained 1,407,671 rows: 985,369 for training, 281,534 for validation, and 140,768 for testing. We applied L2 penalties of 0, $10^{-6}$, $10^{-5}$, and $10^{-4}$ to the hidden-layer weights and compared them using unpenalized validation MSE. The respective minimum values were 0.001207, 0.001332, 0.001583, and 0.003117. No nonzero penalty reduced validation MSE below that of the zero-penalty model, so all final MLP trainings used $\lambda_{\mathrm{L2}}=0$. 

We did not optimize the network architecture separately for each predictor
configuration or held-out region. Instead, we used the same relatively large
network, with ten hidden layers of 200 units, for all experiments. This
architecture provides substantial flexibility to represent nonlinear
relationships between the predictors and $C$, while keeping the network
design fixed across configurations and spatial tests. We limit overfitting through a separate validation set, learning-rate reduction, early stopping, and restoring the model with the lowest validation MSE. The training and validation histories (Fig.~\ref{fig:training_convergence}) show whether training converged and whether the networks overfit. A full architecture search
would require separate tuning across multiple predictor configurations and
spatial splits and was beyond this study's scope. The chosen architecture
should therefore be viewed as a fixed, flexible modeling choice rather than an
optimized architecture.

The complete histories in Fig.~\ref{fig:training_convergence} show that the training and validation losses decrease and then approach a plateau. Median validation MSE decreases from its initial value by factors of 19.5, 19.4, 37.7, 1.68, 148, and 162 for CFG01--CFG06, respectively. After the early epochs, median validation MSE stays below median training MSE, ending at about half of it for CFG03, CFG05, and CFG06. Training MSE is higher because it is computed during each epoch, with batch normalization using the statistics of each batch rather than the running averages used for validation. The histories therefore provide no clear evidence that the relatively large network is increasingly fitting the training rows at the expense of the validation rows. Validation agreement with the reference $C$ is high for CFG01, CFG02, CFG03, CFG05, and CFG06, while CFG04 shows a much smaller reduction in validation loss and higher validation $C$ RMSE and lower $R_C^2$. However, CFG04 still reduces velocity RMSE relative to uniform $C$ in seven of the ten spatially withheld squares. The main implication is that fitting $C$ well on random validation rows within the training region does not, by itself, predict performance in a spatially separated region. The common architecture and training procedure provide a controlled comparison among predictor configurations, while the spatially withheld tests assess whether the learned relationships produce useful modeled velocities elsewhere.

Each combination of holdout test and predictor configuration (for example, SQ03 with CFG02) uses ten reproducible training runs, which differ in their training/validation row assignments, initial weights, and epoch shuffles. The ten squares use all six configurations. The two regional tests use three configurations each, chosen before training by how well the withheld region's predictor values are covered by the training data (the marginal and joint support described in Appendix section~\nameref{app:predictor_population}): CFG01--CFG03 for PIG and CFG04--CFG06 for the inter-catchment corridors, so that each regional test uses the configurations least likely to fail simply because its predictor values lie outside the training range. This gives 66 combinations and 660 MLPs in total. For each combination, we take the median of the ten predicted $C$ fields at each control point. The ten individual runs show how sensitive the predictions are to the training procedure and are combined into the median; they are not independent spatial tests or estimates of predictive uncertainty.

\section{Predicting where transfer succeeds}
\label{app:transfer_predictability}

The preceding sections examine, one at a time, how predictor support, training density, agreement with the reference $C$, and the BedMachine reconstruction relate to velocity performance. Here we test whether the predictors, taken together, can show in
advance where the predicted $C$ improves modeled velocity compared with
uniform $C$.

For each observation-grid row (one MEaSUREs 450~m grid cell) in a withheld
region, we recorded two outcomes. The first, the improvement criterion, is
whether the predicted $C$ gives a lower local velocity error than uniform $C$,
$e_{\mathrm{ML}}<e_{\mathrm{uniform}}$, where $e$ is the local vector-error
magnitude defined in the Methods. The second, the halving criterion,
$e_{\mathrm{ML}}<0.5\,e_{\mathrm{uniform}}$, requires the predicted $C$ to at
least halve the local error; it checks that the results do not depend on
counting small improvements as success.

We then trained random forest classifiers \citep{breiman2001random} to predict
each outcome from local inputs; a random forest can capture nonlinear
relationships among many inputs without specifying their form. The classifiers
predict the outcome rather than $C$, so observed speed, which is excluded from
the MLP predictors, can be used as an input. We compared three sets of inputs,
each with its own classifier: observed speed alone, the twelve predictors, and
the twelve predictors together with observed speed. These input sets are the
same for every configuration, so they include all twelve predictors even when
the MLP used only some of them. All classifiers use the
same settings: 120 trees, each at most 14 splits deep, at least 40 rows in each
final group, and the two outcomes weighted equally.

We tested each classifier on a region it had not seen. For the squares, we
trained on the central squares of nine tests and tested on the central square
of the tenth, repeating this for every square and configuration. Each training
row therefore comes from a predicted $C$ field produced without that location. For PIG, we trained on the central
squares of all ten tests and tested CFG02 over the whole catchment. We left out
the 18,409 training rows that lie inside PIG, so the classifier never sees the
tested area.

We report the percentage of test rows that each classifier labels correctly
and compare it with two simple rules that give the same answer at every row of
the tested region, a standard reference for classification accuracy known as
the no-information rate \citep[Chapter~11]{kuhn2013applied}. The first rule
gives the outcome that is more common in the tested region itself: if the
predicted $C$ improves on uniform $C$ at 70\% of its rows, it answers
``improves'' everywhere and is 70\% correct. It requires the test outcomes,
which are not known in advance. The second
rule gives the outcome that is more common in the training squares, whatever
happens in the tested region; like the classifiers, it uses only the training
data. To show that it can identify where the predicted $C$ succeeds within a region, a classifier must label a higher percentage of rows correctly than both rules.

The ten squares, six MLP configurations, three classifier input sets, and two
criteria (improvement and halving) give 360 tests. In some squares the outcome was the same at every
row, so there was nothing for a classifier to separate. This happened for 10
combinations of square and configuration under the improvement criterion and
12 under the halving criterion; in SQ04, for example, the predicted $C$
improved on uniform $C$ at every row for all six configurations. Leaving out
these combinations, with their three input sets, removes 66 tests and leaves
294. Of the 60 combinations of square and configuration, 50 remain under the
improvement criterion and 48 under the halving criterion.

Across these 294 tests, the classifiers labeled 1,967,017 of 3,626,370 test
rows correctly (54.2\%), and the median score of a test was 51.4\%. The first
rule, which gives the tested square's more common outcome at every row, had a
median of 77.5\%, and the classifiers did better than it in only 32 of the 294
tests. The second rule, which gives the training squares' more common outcome,
had a median of 70.0\%, and the classifiers did better than it in 98 of the 294
tests.

These failures are not a matter of the classifier fitting its training data
poorly. The same classifier labeled 98.7\% of its training rows correctly, and
98.3\% of rows held out at random from within the training squares, compared
with about half of the rows in a spatially separate square. Nor do they depend
on where the classifier's output is cut into a yes or no decision. Choosing
that cut-off on rows held out from the training squares, using only training data,
raised the median to 54.1\% and did better than the first rule in 29 of the
294 tests. Even choosing the best possible cut-off for each withheld square
after seeing its outcomes raised the median to 81.6\% and did better than
the first rule in only 122 of the 294 tests.

The classifiers also failed to transfer to PIG, where 227,685 rows were
evaluated. For the improvement criterion they labeled 30.8\%, 36.4\% and
29.9\% of rows correctly for the three input sets, against 77.6\% for the first
rule. For the halving criterion they labeled 64.2\%, 90.8\% and
89.7\% correctly, against 92.0\%. No input set did better than the first rule
in either case.

Part of the reason is that the relationship between ice speed and success is
reversed between the two settings. In the rows used to train the PIG
classifier, the predicted $C$ meets the improvement criterion at 98.5\% of rows
in the slowest quarter of speeds but at only 48.3\% and 52.1\% in the two
fastest quarters. In PIG, it is met at 51.3\% of rows in the slowest quarter of
PIG speeds and 93.8\% in the fastest. A classifier that learns where the
predicted $C$ succeeds in the squares therefore gets the pattern backwards in
PIG.

The predictors therefore do not provide a reliable way to identify in advance
where the predicted $C$ will improve modeled velocity. The combined classifier,
which uses observed speed and the twelve predictors, is not consistently better
than the one using observed speed alone, rarely does better than the first
rule, falls short of the second rule in most tests, and does not transfer from
the squares to PIG. The same classifier labels its training squares almost perfectly, so the
difficulty lies in transferring to a new region, not in the classifier's
ability to fit the data. The tests in which every row had the same outcome point the same way: whether
the point-wise relationship succeeds depends mainly on the withheld region as
a whole rather than on individual locations within it. Together with the tests of predictor support and training density, this
indicates that the local predictor values, individually or together, do not
determine where the predicted $C$ succeeds. If $C$ were a point-wise function
of these predictors (Eqn~\ref{eq:functional_relationship}), failures would be
tied to particular predictor values; instead, they depend on the region, which
does not support the point-wise hypothesis.


\section{Sensitivity to the replacement region}
\label{app:replacement_sensitivity}

For each square, predicted and uniform $C$ are applied only within the complete 130~km
excluded footprint where $\phi>0.1$. The PIG experiment uses the corresponding catchment mask. Elsewhere, including across the grounding transition, we retain the inversion-derived reference $C$. This keeps the geometry,
boundary conditions, and grounding treatment identical while holding the
surrounding reference control fixed.

We also compared the controlled design with an earlier calculation in which
the predicted $C$, or the corresponding uniform-$C$ value, replaced the
inversion-reference control throughout the common eligible grounded sector.
In that earlier design, the predicted control was therefore also used in the
MLP training geography. In the controlled design used for the main results,
replacement is restricted to the complete 130~km footprint for each square,
or to the held-out regional geography, intersected with the same common
eligible grounded mask. Predicted and uniform controls use identical masks,
and both retain inversion-reference $C$ outside the mask. The complete 40~km
buffer around each central square is part of the replacement region.

We evaluated the effects of correcting the observational reference and changing the replacement geography separately. Replacing the
mesh-interpolated observational values with the original paired MEaSUREs
components changed the central-square MLP RMSE by a median of
$0.07\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$ and a maximum of
$0.95\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$. Restricting replacement to the withheld
geography then changed the central-square MLP RMSE by a median of
$0.003\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$ and a maximum of
$2.02\,\mathrm{m\,a^{-1}}$. Neither change altered whether a configuration
improved on uniform $C$ in any square or which configuration had the lowest
RMSE in a square. Differences were larger over the complete footprints. For
CFG02 and CFG04, the median relative RMSE changed from 0.54 and 0.82 under
sector-wide replacement to 0.64 and 0.86 under controlled replacement; both
configurations improved on uniform $C$ in nine of ten complete footprints in
both designs. Across these 20 footprint comparisons, the median absolute
change in relative RMSE was 0.04 and the largest was 0.28. The principal
central-square findings are therefore robust to the two replacement designs,
while the complete-footprint results are more sensitive to the surrounding
control prescription.
